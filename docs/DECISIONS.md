## Locked decisions

### Point 1: Research question — Option C, with fallback to Option B

The question: when a robot gets a semantic prior map that is wrong a known fraction of the time, and whose confidence values may be dishonest, does a PPO policy learn the right amount of trust, and how close is that trust to the optimum? Two independent dials: corruption level ρ (each episode draws a true reliability r from a Beta distribution with mean 1 − ρ; with probability r the object is where the map says, otherwise in another plausible room) and calibration factor κ (displayed confidence c = σ(κ·logit(r)); κ = 1 calibrated, κ > 1 overconfident, κ < 1 underconfident). Both dials are built from day one; the corruption experiment is the result that must exist; the miscalibration experiment can shrink to evaluation-only, or be dropped, falling back to Option B (corruption only).

Plain-words meaning of ρ and r (explained 2026-11-16): ρ is the experiment dial, "on average how often is the map wrong", and the x-axis of E1; 1 − ρ is the map's average reliability. r is the reliability of the map in one episode, drawn fresh each episode near 1 − ρ (factory analogy: ρ is the factory's overall fault rate, r is today's batch). r varies so that c varies within a ρ condition, which is what lets the policy learn and show trust by confidence level.

Why: it builds on Khaled's thesis knowledge of confidence-weighted semantic maps, gives the strongest contribution, and stays interesting even if PPO does not beat the heuristics. Rejected Option A (performance only) because its only result is a number that can easily lose to heuristics in small worlds.

### Point 2: Simulator — Option B: train in a fast 2D floor-plan simulator, test and demo in Gazebo

Training happens in a 2D floor-plan simulator we write ourselves: metric rooms and doors, A* moves the robot between the places the policy chooses, thousands of episodes per minute on CPU. The trained policy is frozen and tested in Gazebo. The policy learns in terms of room features (prior says target here, confidence, path distance, already searched), never room IDs, so it carries over to new houses.

Why: Castelo-Branco et al. (arXiv 2603.25366, 2026), the closest paper, trained and tested inside Habitat 3.0, which limited them to two scenes and a separate DQN per scene; they list cross-scene generalization and sim-to-real as limitations and future work. Our design trains one policy across many generated houses and tests on unseen houses, plus gives an impressive, non-abstract demo. Rejected MiniGrid-only (abstract, weak demo, wastes the robotics background) and training directly in Gazebo (each episode about a minute, days per run).

Gazebo test world — Option A: our own houses exported from the 2D simulator into SDF (box walls, floor color by room type, simple box furniture), with a minimal two-wheeled robot with lidar and Gazebo's differential-drive plugin, driven by Nav2 through the ros_gz bridge. No TurtleBot needed. Why: lightest, works with any new Gazebo version, the Gazebo map matches the 2D map exactly, and it allows several unseen test houses. Rejected the AWS RoboMaker Small House (its new-Gazebo port needs Harmonic/Jazzy, heavier, single house), the Webots apartment (new simulator, known Nav2/Fast-DDS issue on Humble, single house) and the TurtleBot3 House (new-Gazebo version only on Jazzy).

### Cost of a decision — Option C

The reward penalizes the planned distance in meters (A* path on the occupancy map) of the rooms the policy chose. Nav2's driving error is never penalized or trained on. Gazebo results are reported in two rows: decision metrics computed on planned distance (success, SPL on planned paths, decision regret, agreement with the optimal room choice), which is the trust result, and end-to-end metrics on actually driven distance (plus driving overhead and Nav2 failure rate), which is a realism check. If Nav2 fails a goal, retry once; if it fails again, log a navigation failure that counts only in the end-to-end row.

Why: the research question is about trust, not navigation, and mixing navigation error into training would create a confound. This matches Castelo-Branco's training rule (their per-segment reward is n_prim·(−0.01) + 1_succ·(+1), counting executed primitives in a deterministic simulator), plus an extra row they could not produce. We count meters rather than steps because our simulator has no fixed-size steps.

### Point 3a: House layouts — Option C (our own ProcTHOR-style room-spec generator), fixed at 5 rooms

The generator: a rectangular footprint (optionally with one or two rectangular corner cuts for L/T shapes) is split recursively following a room spec, a small tree that groups rooms into a public zone (living room, kitchen) and a private zone (bedrooms, bathroom). Each room has a growth weight that sets its share of its zone's area. Door rules follow ProcTHOR: the bathroom gets one door, only kitchen–living pairs may have an open connection, the entrance is in the kitchen or living room. Houses that break an adjacency rule are rejected and redrawn (rejection sampling). Method citations: Deitke et al., "ProcTHOR: Large-Scale Embodied AI Using Procedural Generation", NeurIPS 2022 (arXiv 2206.06994), and Lopes et al., "A constrained growth method for procedural floor plan generation", Game-ON 2010. Design constraints carried over from the 3a discussion: distances must vary (the trust decision trades confidence against distance) and doors are 1.0 m wide so Nav2's inflation margin does not block them.

Every house has exactly 5 searchable rooms (Khaled's choice). The 5 room types vary between a few room specs (set in 3b), so a row position never stands for a fixed room type. The start room counts as already searched, so an episode has at most 4 decisions. The exact optimum is tiny: current room × searched set = 5·2^5 = 160 states.

Pools fixed by seed range: about 5,000 training houses (seeds 0–4,999), 200 validation (from 100,000), 200 test (from 200,000). Each house is saved with its occupancy grid and a precomputed table of planned distances between every pair of room viewpoints (A* and Dijkstra give the same shortest length on the same grid, so training does lookups; A* is still used when path geometry is needed). Extra held-out test: about 100 ProcTHOR-10K houses with exactly 5 rooms (Apache 2.0, `pip install prior`, `prior.load_dataset("procthor-10k")`), 2D only, evaluated once at the end as an extra E1 row; dropped if the converter takes more than about 2 hours. Two or three of our own test houses are exported to Gazebo. Development starts in one fixed house and switches to the pool once the loop works. Fallback: if Option C takes more than about half a day, use Option B (recursive splitting) with rule-based room labels.

Why Option C: realistic topology is needed for 3b's "plausible other room" and for the distance-dependent threshold c*; thousands of layouts guard against memorization (Cobbe et al., "Quantifying Generalization in Reinforcement Learning", ICML 2019: substantial overfitting in CoinRun below 4,000 training levels); a citable generation method; the ProcTHOR row turns "unseen houses" into "unseen houses from another generator", which answers Castelo-Branco's cross-scene limitation. Why fixed 5 rooms: simplicity — fixed-size observation (5 rows) and action space (5 choices), a tiny exact optimum, faster debugging. Note: a fixed count is not required by the policy (a shared per-room scorer works for any count); with a fixed count, the row order must be shuffled per episode (or the network must not depend on order) so the policy cannot learn slot identity. Accepted cost: less layout variety, a smaller spread of distances, and smaller differences between methods in small houses.

Rejected: A, hand-designed templates (too few shapes, memorization risk, weak unseen-house test); B, plain recursive splitting with random types (unrealistic connections such as reaching the kitchen through a bathroom; kept only as fallback); D, ProcTHOR-10K as the only source (only four room types, little control, converter needed; kept as the extra test set); E, HouseExpo and other real-plan datasets (HouseExpo is built on SUNCG, which was withdrawn after Planner 5D's lawsuit; its repository states no license; rooms stored as bounding boxes and doors not given; RPLAN comes as images that need converting).

### Point 3b: Objects and room types (complete, 2026-11-16)

#### Target set — Option B, target categories drawn per episode

Each episode has one target with a single static instance in the house. Only the current target counts for detection and success; seeing other objects gives no reward and does not end the episode. Each category has its own usual rooms in the co-occurrence table. The network does not grow, because the per-room features are relative to the current target (the policy never sees the object's name). Debugging starts with a single target.

Why: with several targets whose usual rooms differ, the map's claim and the object-specific habit agree in some episodes and disagree in others, so trust in the map can be measured separately from habit. Rejected: one fixed target forever (mixes trust in the map with a habit such as "mugs are in kitchens, go to the kitchen") and many categories taken from ProcTHOR placements (too much to check by hand in two weeks).

#### Room types and room specs — Option C: four core rooms plus a variable fifth room (locked 2026-11-16)

Every house has kitchen, living room, bathroom and bedroom. The fifth room is a second bedroom in 50% of houses, an office in 25% (private zone, ordinary door) and a dining room in 25% (public zone, same door rules as the living room). The policy never sees room-type names; the co-occurrence weights are applied to the rooms actually present in each house and renormalized (rescaled to sum to 1), so ProcTHOR houses (only kitchen, living room, bathroom, bedroom, possibly with duplicates) work without changes and act as a mild room-type-shift test.

Why (recorded by Claude, since Khaled gave the choice without a reason; Khaled may correct it): the varying fifth room makes the habit signal change between houses (for example the laptop's most likely room is the office when one exists), which helps separate trust in the map from habit; the second-bedroom spec keeps duplicate rooms in half the houses, where the map is most valuable because habit can say "a bedroom" but not which one; the extra cost is small (two labels, two Gazebo floor colors, 12 more table numbers). Rejected: A, one fixed spec (kitchen, living, bathroom, two bedrooms) because the habit signal would be identical in every episode for a given target; B, two or three specs from only ProcTHOR's four types, because the variety is cosmetic and "one bedroom, two bathrooms" is an unrealistic house.

The eight categories, all real AI2-THOR/ProcTHOR object types, with relative co-occurrence weights (not probabilities; every entry has a floor of 0.5 so no object is ever impossible in any room, which avoids free room-skipping and keeps the value-iteration solver well behaved). Weights are a draft and can be tuned before training starts.

Expected result: the map matters much more for weak-habit targets, visible as a larger PPO-Conf versus PPO-NoPrior gap. Rejected: A, six strong-habit objects (habit dominates and the map adds little); C, the six-target set (mug, remote, towel, laptop, book, keys), which covers the range with fewer numbers but gives only three categories per habit level.

#### Where the object is when the map is wrong — Option B: drawn from the co-occurrence table (locked 2026-11-16)

Claim-first order (from Point 1): the map claims a room; with probability r the object is there; otherwise it is in one of the other rooms of this house, drawn from the table weights for the current target, renormalized over those remaining rooms (0.5 floor). Refined by the 3c start-room lock: "other rooms" excludes both the claimed room and the start room, so a wrong claim puts the object in one of the remaining three rooms. The value-iteration solver and the Bayes baselines use the same table, so the optimum stays exact. A config flag `placement = table | uniform` exists from day one; uniform is just the table with every weight set to 1, so it costs no extra code. One E1 ablation row (an ablation reruns an experiment with one ingredient switched off) is trained and tested with `uniform` to separate trust in the map from commonsense. In the main results PPO-NoPrior becomes the "commonsense only" agent, so the PPO-Conf minus PPO-NoPrior gap is the value the map adds on top of commonsense. Worked example (written before the start-room lock, so it spreads over four rooms): target book, two-bedroom house, map claims living room, r = 0.6; each bedroom gets 0.4 × 4/9.5 ≈ 0.17, bathroom about 0.04, kitchen about 0.02 (uniform would give each 0.10), so after an empty living room each bedroom holds 0.42 of what remains.

Computation (Khaled's concern, answered): sampling the room is one random number per episode (microseconds); value iteration over 160 states × at most 5 actions costs about 1 ms per episode, about 1 s for all 1,000 E1 test episodes, and is needed equally under Option A. The table changes the numbers, not the amount of computation. Dependence on the table is bounded: the table only acts in the 1 − r share of episodes where the map is wrong, the uniform flag always gives the table-free version, and the ablation row turns "why these numbers?" into a measured result. Optional at any time without changing this lock: debug with the table of ones first (the staged Option C that was offered).

Why (Khaled's reason, corrected and recorded): Khaled said "it now learns commonsense". Correction: Option B puts realistic commonsense regularities into the world, but the policy can only learn to use them if Point 4 shows them to it. Recorded reason: with Option B the world contains realistic commonsense regularities, so the policy can learn to combine two knowledge sources, the map's claim and commonsense about where the object usually is, deciding from experience how much weight each deserves; this requires Point 4 to include, per room, that room's table weight for the current target, normalized over the house (done in 4a). Rejected: A, uniform other room (unrealistic, wastes the realistic topology, makes PPO-NoPrior nearly identical to the nearest-room explorer; kept as the ablation setting); C, staged (uniform until the 20 November checkpoint, then table), not chosen as the plan, though the flag keeps it available.

Clarifications discussed after the lock (2026-11-16). Habit does not override the map by design: in claim-first order the claimed room holds the object with probability r, so for the towel in a two-bedroom house with the map claiming the living room, P(living) = c and P(bathroom) = 0.615(1 − c) (computed over four other rooms, before the start-room lock; with the start room excluded the bathroom share rises slightly, for example to 8/12 ≈ 0.67 of 1 − c when the robot starts in the kitchen, moving the crossover from about 0.38 to about 0.40); the map wins above that crossover (lower still when the claimed room is closer). A policy that always goes to the bathroom for towels makes a measurable mistake (visible in regret and in the E2 prior-swap test); the target behavior is "follow the map when confident, fall back to habit when not". Learning "towels are usually in bathrooms" is not harmful memorization, because the rule holds in every training and test house. The confidence c and the habit weights are different quantities: c is per claim and per episode, attached to one room, and can be dishonest when κ ≠ 1; the habit weight is per target and room type, covers all rooms, and never lies; they agree in some episodes and disagree in others, and after the claimed room is found empty only habit can rank the remaining rooms. They are kept as separate inputs rather than merged into one posterior per room, because merging assumes c is honest (that is Bayes-nominal) and would remove the policy's chance to learn to correct miscalibration (H3).

### Point 3c: How the prior map is produced and corrupted (complete, 2026-11-16)

#### How the claimed room is chosen — Option C: mixture of table and uniform with λ = 0.5 (locked 2026-11-16)

Each episode, a coin with probability λ = 0.5 decides how the claimed room is picked: with probability 1 − λ it is drawn from the current target's table weights over this house's five rooms; with probability λ it is drawn uniformly (each room 0.2). This λ coin only affects what the map says; where the object goes when the claim is wrong is always the table (3b). λ is a fixed design setting reported in Methods, not learned (structurally like ε-greedy from M4-3, but used by the environment to generate hints, not by the agent to pick actions; not related to Double Q-learning, which keeps two learned Q-tables). λ = 0 recovers Option B and λ = 1 recovers Option A, so both remain available without new code; an E1 sensitivity row varying λ (for example 0, 0.5, 1) checks whether the main conclusion survives a different error model.

Numbers for the towel in a two-bedroom house (claim distribution): pure table gives bathroom 0.59, each bedroom 0.15, kitchen 0.07, living room 0.04; λ = 0.5 gives bathroom 0.40, each bedroom 0.17, kitchen 0.14, living room 0.12. Sharp disagreements (map claims a room habit rates as unlikely: kitchen or living room) rise from 11% to 26%. Correction recorded: under the pure table the map already points away from the bathroom 41% of the time, so "almost always agree" overstated the problem; the real issue is that sharp disagreements, which test the crossover, are rare.

Why (recorded by Claude, since Khaled gave the choice without a reason; Khaled may correct it): Option C is the only option that keeps habit meaningful and also produces enough sharp map-versus-habit disagreements to train and test the crossover; λ = 0 and λ = 1 recover the other two options for a sensitivity row. Rejected: A, uniform claims (because the object sits in the claimed room with probability r, at low ρ objects would follow the claims and be spread uniformly, making the table and the 3b lock pointless); B, claims from the table only (realistic, but sharp disagreements are rare, about 11% for the towel, so the policy sees few cases where the map should beat strong habit and trust becomes hard to separate from habit).

Khaled's concern "aren't we relying too much on probabilities?" (answered): an unreliable map is itself a probability statement, so the randomness is the research question, not a side effect; it is confined to the draws at reset; the known generator is what allows the exact value-iteration optimum and regret, which Castelo-Branco could not compute in Habitat. Limitation for the Discussion: real semantic map errors are not generated by this Beta, λ and table recipe, so conclusions hold for this error model; every dial (ρ, κ, λ, table versus uniform) is a single number with an off switch, setting λ = 1 with the uniform table gives the simplest world for debugging, and "real map error models" is stated future work linked to the thesis.

#### How many rooms the map claims — Option A: a single claimed room with one confidence c (locked 2026-11-16)

The map says exactly one thing per episode, for example "towel: living room, 0.55": one claimed room, one true reliability r, one displayed confidence c, one coin deciding whether the claim is right. The other rooms are ranked only by the habit weights.

Why (recorded by Claude, since Khaled gave the choice without a reason; Khaled may correct it): it matches the Point 1 dials exactly; the threshold c* stays a single, explainable number; it keeps the map and habit as clearly separate knowledge sources, which 3b was built around; it is a recognizable simplification of real semantic maps, many of which store for each place the most likely label plus its confidence. Full-distribution maps are stated future work linked to the thesis. Rejected: B, a full probability per room (closest to real semantic maps, but needs a separate model of how the whole vector goes wrong, κ must become a temperature on five numbers, r loses its single meaning, c* stops being one threshold, and the map's numbers for the other rooms duplicate the habit weights, merging map and habit); C, top-2 claims with two confidences (a second coin and a second confidence in every feature row for little gain in what can be measured).

#### Spread of r — Option B: Beta concentration n = 10 (locked 2026-11-16)

r ~ Beta(α, β) with α = 10(1 − ρ) and β = 10ρ, so the mean is 1 − ρ. A Beta distribution is a distribution over numbers between 0 and 1; α and β act like counts of past successes and failures, and n = α + β sets how tightly r clusters around the mean. At ρ = 0.4 this is Beta(6, 4): standard deviation about 0.15, r mostly between 0.45 and 0.75. At ρ = 0.8 it is Beta(2, 8). ρ = 0 is handled as r = 1 (a perfect map, c = 1), since β = 0 is not a valid Beta and logit(1) is infinite.

Why (recorded by Claude, since Khaled chose the recommended option without a reason; Khaled may correct it): the ρ conditions stay distinct, so the E1 curves keep their shape, while c still varies inside each condition, so trust by confidence level can be learned and measured in E2. Rejected: A, no spread (r = 1 − ρ exactly; every claim in a test condition looks identical, E2 confidence bins collapse at fixed ρ, and it contradicts the Point 1 lock); C, n = 2 (at ρ = 0.4, Beta(1.2, 0.8), standard deviation about 0.28, r spans almost 0 to 1, neighboring ρ conditions overlap heavily and the E1 curves flatten). An interactive dot plot of sampled r values for the three options was shown in chat.

#### Start-room rule — Option C: start drawn before the object, among the four non-claimed rooms (locked 2026-11-16)

The start room is drawn uniformly among the four rooms other than the claimed room, before the object is placed; the start room counts as already searched. If the r coin says the claim is correct, the object is in the claimed room; otherwise it is placed among the remaining three rooms (not the claimed room, not the start room) by the table weights renormalized over them.

Why (recorded by Claude, since Khaled chose the recommended option without a reason; Khaled may correct it): no episode is won or settled at time zero, every episode has a real first decision, and P(claim correct) = r exactly, so the exact optimum stays exact. Rejected: A, start anywhere (some episodes are won at time zero, and when the start is the claimed room the claim is settled before any decision); B, start anywhere except the object's room and the claimed room, drawn after placement (leaks information: a given start room is chosen with probability 1/4 when the claim is correct but 1/3 when it is wrong, so the start position itself tilts the odds toward "map wrong"; this was Claude's own earlier proposal, corrected before locking).

#### Final episode generation recipe (Point 3 complete)

All randomness happens at reset; after it everything is deterministic (precomputed distances, deterministic camera, reward = minus meters). 1) Draw a house from the pool. 2) Draw a target uniformly from the eight. 3) Draw r ~ Beta(10(1 − ρ), 10ρ) (r = 1 if ρ = 0) and display c = σ(κ·logit(r)). 4) Flip the λ coin and pick the claimed room (table weights or uniform). 5) Draw the start room uniformly among the four non-claimed rooms. 6) Flip the r coin: if correct, the object is in the claimed room; otherwise draw it among the remaining three rooms by the table. Worked example: house 1,742, towel, ρ = 0.4 gives r = 0.55 and c = 0.55 at κ = 1; λ coin lands uniform and the die picks the living room; start room is the kitchen; r coin lands wrong; the remaining weights bathroom 8, bedroom A 2, bedroom B 2 (sum 12) put the towel in the bathroom with 8/12 ≈ 0.67, each bedroom 0.17; it lands in the bathroom. The robot knows only: map says living room with 0.55, the habit weights, distances, and that the kitchen is already searched.

### Point 4: Observation (complete, 2026-11-17)

#### 4a: Per-room feature row — Option A: minimal five-feature row, no global features (locked 2026-11-17)

The observation is 5 rows, one per room, in an order shuffled every episode, never containing room IDs or room-type names. Each row has five numbers: 1) planned distance (A* on the occupancy map) from the robot's current room viewpoint to this room's viewpoint, divided by the largest room-to-room planned distance in this house, so it lies in [0, 1]; 2) searched flag (0 or 1); 3) claimed flag (1 only on the room the map claims); 4) displayed confidence c on the claimed row, 0 on every other row; 5) this room's co-occurrence weight for the current target, normalized over the rooms of this house (habit). c and habit stay separate inputs (see the 3b clarifications). No global features (such as rooms searched so far or meters spent): the true state is current room plus searched set, which the rows already contain, so the Markov property holds. Revisit only if Point 6 introduces a distance budget, in which case "meters spent" or "budget left" becomes part of the state.

Why (Khaled's reason): simplicity, and no need to complicate the project further. Rejected: B, adding global features (only needed with a budget; otherwise extra inputs the policy must learn to ignore); C, also adding each room's distance to the claimed room (helps "on the way" route planning but is partly redundant, since the network can combine distances itself).

#### 4b: Rooms only driven through — Option A: they do not count as searched (locked 2026-11-17)

Only the room the policy chose gets searched; the virtual camera checks for the target only on arrival at that room's viewpoint, in both the 2D simulator and Gazebo. Rooms the robot passes through stay unsearched. In the Gazebo demo this is explained as "the robot searches only where it decides to look".

Why (Khaled's reason): keep it simple, as in 4a; it also keeps the exact optimum exact, since the value-iteration state stays current room plus searched set and the next state depends only on the chosen room. Rejected: B, transit counts as searching (more realistic, but the solver needs per-path room coverage and partial-coverage rules); C, transit detection ends the episode but does not mark rooms searched (natural demo, but gives the policy free detections the solver does not count, so regret is no longer exact).

#### 4c: How the comparison variants are formed — Option A (locked 2026-11-17)

All three PPO agents share the same network and code and differ only by a fixed mask that zeroes inputs (inputs are zeroed, not deleted). PPO-Conf ("confidence"): sees everything, the claimed room and its confidence c. PPO-Binary: sees the claimed flag in every episode but c is always 0, so it knows where the map points but never how sure the map is; it can only learn one average level of trust over the training mix of ρ. PPO-NoPrior ("no prior map"): claimed flag and c both always 0; it sees only distances, searched flags and habit weights, so it is the "commonsense only" agent. The names describe information removed step by step: claim plus confidence, claim only, nothing. PPO-Conf minus PPO-Binary measures the value of the confidence number; PPO-Binary minus PPO-NoPrior measures the value of the claim itself. PPO is Proximal Policy Optimization: a neural network outputs the policy directly (the probability of choosing each room), and each update changes the policy only a little from the previous one to keep training stable.

Why (Khaled's reason): the most logical choice, and it does not leak confidence information for the model to use. Rejected: B, show the claim only when c ≥ 0.5 (mimics real thresholded semantic maps but leaks one bit of confidence, so the PPO-Conf versus PPO-Binary gap no longer measures the full value of c; can be mentioned in the Discussion as a realistic middle case); C, claimed flag with c fixed at the training average (about 0.6; the same information as A in a different form).

### Agreements that follow from these decisions

Gazebo results never update the policy. If simulator calibration is ever needed, Gazebo work is split into a calibration set (about 20 Nav2 goals without the policy) and a test set that is never fed back. Checkpoints are selected on a validation set of 2D houses (separate seed range from training and test). Observations are computed by identical code in both simulators (same A* on the same occupancy map, shared room and viewpoint points). Detection is a virtual camera: a field-of-view cone plus line-of-sight ray casting on the occupancy map, the same function in both simulators, using Gazebo's ground-truth pose. The task is find only, no manipulation; success means the target is visible within about 1.5 m with clear line of sight. One decision is one step (a semi-MDP); γ of 0.99 or 1 per decision, to confirm at Point 7. Domain randomization of driving is out of the core plan; it returns only as Discussion material if the end-to-end gap is large. In Gazebo the house geometry is fixed per test house, but each episode changes the target, the object positions, the prior map, the confidence and the start pose. "Explore" has two meanings to keep distinct in the presentation: searching rooms the map does not point to (task sense) and trying non-greedy actions during training (RL sense).

Added 2026-11-16: drawing a house per episode from a fixed pool is part of the initial-state distribution, so the MDP stays fixed and stationary (like the dealt cards in Sutton and Barto's Blackjack example); the house is visible to the policy through its features, so the Markov property holds. Every method, seed and ρ value is evaluated on the identical test houses and episode seeds (paired evaluation, also called common random numbers). Optional extra result: a policy trained in one house and tested on unseen houses, to show memorization directly. Hints are random per episode but structured: r drawn from Beta(10(1 − ρ), 10ρ), the claim correct with probability r, c = σ(κ·logit(r)); κ is fixed per training run or test condition, not per episode; the claimed room is chosen by the λ mixture, and the map claims exactly one room (3c). The policy learns trust from returns: c is an input, so different confidence levels are different states (like a separate bandit per confidence level), the meter-based reward makes the threshold c* distance-dependent, and the policy's own random sampling (RL exploration) is what lets it discover that distrust pays at low confidence.

Consequence for Point 5: actions must be high level (room-level "go search room k"), not primitive grid moves. To decide at Point 5: what happens when the policy picks an already-searched room (masking, penalty, or allowed), and whether "declare found" is an explicit action or success is automatic on detection at the viewpoint.

Consequence for Points 6 and 9: the target always exists and detection is deterministic, so any agent that searches every room eventually succeeds; success rate alone says little. The quality measures are decision regret and SPL, or success within a distance or decision budget set at Point 6. If Point 6 adds a budget, revisit the 4a decision on global features.
