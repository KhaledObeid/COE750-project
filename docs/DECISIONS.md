## Locked decisions

### Point 1: Research question — Option C, with fallback to Option B

The question: when a robot gets a semantic prior map that is wrong a known fraction of the time, and whose confidence values may be dishonest, does a PPO policy learn the right amount of trust, and how close is that trust to the optimum? Two independent dials: corruption level ρ (each episode draws a true reliability r from a Beta distribution with mean 1 − ρ; with probability r the object is where the map says, otherwise in another plausible room) and calibration factor κ (displayed confidence c = σ(κ·logit(r)); κ = 1 calibrated, κ > 1 overconfident, κ < 1 underconfident). Both dials are built from day one; the corruption experiment is the result that must exist; the miscalibration experiment can shrink to evaluation-only, or be dropped, falling back to Option B (corruption only).

Plain-words meaning of ρ and r (explained 2026-11-16): ρ is the experiment dial, "on average how often is the map wrong", and the x-axis of E1; 1 − ρ is the map's average reliability. r is the reliability of the map in one episode, drawn fresh each episode near 1 − ρ (factory analogy: ρ is the factory's overall fault rate, r is today's batch). r varies so that c varies within a ρ condition, which is what lets the policy learn and show trust by confidence level.

Why: it builds on Khaled's thesis knowledge of confidence-weighted semantic maps, gives the strongest contribution, and stays interesting even if PPO does not beat the heuristics. Rejected Option A (performance only) because its only result is a number that can easily lose to heuristics in small worlds.

### Point 2: Simulator — Option B: train in a fast 2D floor-plan simulator, test and demo in Gazebo

Training happens in a 2D floor-plan simulator we write ourselves: metric rooms and doors, A* moves the robot between the places the policy chooses, thousands of episodes per minute on CPU. The trained policy is frozen and tested in Gazebo. The policy learns in terms of room features (prior says target here, confidence, path distance, already searched), never room IDs, so it carries over to new houses.

Why: Castelo-Branco et al. (arXiv 2603.25366, 2026), the closest paper, trained and tested inside Habitat 3.0, which limited them to two scenes and a separate DQN per scene; they list cross-scene generalization and sim-to-real as limitations and future work. Our design trains one policy across many generated houses and tests on unseen houses, plus gives an impressive, non-abstract demo. Rejected MiniGrid-only (abstract, weak demo, wastes the robotics background) and training directly in Gazebo (each episode about a minute, days per run).

Gazebo test world — Option A: our own houses exported from the 2D simulator into SDF (box walls, floor color by room type, simple box furniture), with a minimal two-wheeled robot with lidar and Gazebo's differential-drive plugin, driven by Nav2 through the ros_gz bridge. No TurtleBot needed. Why: lightest, works with any new Gazebo version, the Gazebo map matches the 2D map exactly, and it allows several unseen test houses. Rejected the AWS RoboMaker Small House (its new-Gazebo port needs Harmonic/Jazzy, heavier, single house), the Webots apartment (new simulator, known Nav2/Fast-DDS issue on Humble, single house) and the TurtleBot3 House (new-Gazebo version only on Jazzy).

### Cost of a decision — Option C

The reward penalizes the planned distance in meters (A* path on the occupancy map) of the rooms the policy chose. Nav2's driving error is never penalized or trained on. Gazebo results are reported in two rows: decision metrics computed on planned distance (success, SPL on planned paths, decision regret, agreement with the optimal room choice), which is the trust result, and end-to-end metrics on actually driven distance (plus driving overhead and Nav2 failure rate), which is a realism check. If Nav2 fails a goal, retry once; if it fails again, log a navigation failure that counts only in the end-to-end row.

Why: the research question is about trust, not navigation, and mixing navigation error into training would create a confound. This matches Castelo-Branco's training rule (their per-segment reward is n_prim·(−0.01) + 1_succ·(+1), counting executed primitives in a deterministic simulator), plus an extra row they could not produce. We count meters rather than steps because our simulator has no fixed-size steps.

### Point 3a: House layouts — Option C (our own ProcTHOR-style room-spec generator), fixed at 5 rooms

The generator: a rectangular footprint (optionally with one or two rectangular corner cuts for L/T shapes) is split recursively following a room spec, a small tree that groups rooms into a public zone (living room, kitchen) and a private zone (bedrooms, bathroom). Each room has a growth weight that sets its share of its zone's area. Door rules follow ProcTHOR: the bathroom gets one door, only kitchen–living pairs may have an open connection, the entrance is in the kitchen or living room. Houses that break an adjacency rule are rejected and redrawn (rejection sampling). Method citations: Deitke et al., "ProcTHOR: Large-Scale Embodied AI Using Procedural Generation", NeurIPS 2022 (arXiv 2206.06994), and Lopes et al., "A constrained growth method for procedural floor plan generation", Game-ON 2010. Design constraints carried over from the 3a discussion: distances must vary (the trust decision trades confidence against distance) and doors are 1.0 m wide so Nav2's inflation margin does not block them.

Every house has exactly 5 searchable rooms (Khaled's choice). The 5 room types vary between a few room specs (set in 3b), so a row position never stands for a fixed room type. The start room counts as already searched, so an episode has at most 4 decisions (reduced to a cap of 3 in 6b). The exact optimum is tiny: current room × searched set = 5·2^5 = 160 states.

Pools fixed by seed range: about 5,000 training houses (seeds 0–4,999), 200 validation (from 100,000), 200 test (from 200,000). Each house is saved with its occupancy grid and a precomputed table of planned distances between every pair of room viewpoints (A* and Dijkstra give the same shortest length on the same grid, so training does lookups; A* is still used when path geometry is needed). Extra held-out test: about 100 ProcTHOR-10K houses with exactly 5 rooms (Apache 2.0, `pip install prior`, `prior.load_dataset("procthor-10k")`), 2D only, evaluated once at the end as an extra E1 row; dropped if the converter takes more than about 2 hours. Two or three of our own test houses are exported to Gazebo. Development starts in one fixed house and switches to the pool once the loop works. Fallback: if Option C takes more than about half a day, use Option B (recursive splitting) with rule-based room labels.

Why Option C: realistic topology is needed for 3b's "plausible other room" and for the distance-dependent threshold c*; thousands of layouts guard against memorization (Cobbe et al., "Quantifying Generalization in Reinforcement Learning", ICML 2019: substantial overfitting in CoinRun below 4,000 training levels); a citable generation method; the ProcTHOR row turns "unseen houses" into "unseen houses from another generator", which answers Castelo-Branco's cross-scene limitation. Why fixed 5 rooms: simplicity — fixed-size observation (5 rows) and action space (5 choices), a tiny exact optimum, faster debugging. Note: a fixed count is not required by the policy (a shared per-room scorer works for any count); with a fixed count, the row order must be shuffled per episode (or the network must not depend on order) so the policy cannot learn slot identity. Accepted cost: less layout variety, a smaller spread of distances, and smaller differences between methods in small houses.

Rejected: A, hand-designed templates (too few shapes, memorization risk, weak unseen-house test); B, plain recursive splitting with random types (unrealistic connections such as reaching the kitchen through a bathroom; kept only as fallback); D, ProcTHOR-10K as the only source (only four room types, little control, converter needed; kept as the extra test set); E, HouseExpo and other real-plan datasets (HouseExpo is built on SUNCG, which was withdrawn after Planner 5D's lawsuit; its repository states no license; rooms stored as bounding boxes and doors not given; RPLAN comes as images that need converting).

### Point 3b: Objects and room types (complete, 2026-11-16)

#### Target set — Option B, target categories drawn per episode

Each episode has one target with a single static instance in the house. Only the current target counts for detection and success; seeing other objects gives no reward and does not end the episode. Each category has its own usual rooms in the co-occurrence table. The network does not grow, because the per-room features are relative to the current target (the policy never sees the object's name). Debugging starts with a single target.

Why: with several targets whose usual rooms differ, the map's claim and the object-specific habit agree in some episodes and disagree in others, so trust in the map can be measured separately from habit. Rejected: one fixed target forever (mixes trust in the map with a habit such as "mugs are in kitchens, go to the kitchen") and many categories taken from ProcTHOR placements (too much to check by hand in two weeks).

#### Room types and room specs — Option C: four core rooms plus a variable fifth room (locked 2026-11-16)

Every house has kitchen, living room, bathroom and bedroom. The fifth room is a second bedroom in 50% of houses, an office in 25% (private zone, ordinary door) and a dining room in 25% (public zone, same door rules as the living room). The policy never sees room-type names; the co-occurrence weights are applied to the rooms actually present in each house and renormalized (rescaled to sum to 1), so ProcTHOR houses (only kitchen, living room, bathroom, bedroom, possibly with duplicates) work without changes and act as a mild room-type-shift test.

Why (recorded by Claude, since Khaled gave the choice without a reason; Khaled may correct it): the varying fifth room makes the habit signal change between houses (for example the laptop's most likely room is the office when one exists), which helps separate trust in the map from habit; the second-bedroom spec keeps duplicate rooms in half the houses, where the map is most valuable because habit can say "a bedroom" but not which one; the extra cost is small (two labels, two Gazebo floor colors, 12 more table numbers). Rejected: A, one fixed spec (kitchen, living, bathroom, two bedrooms) because the habit signal would be identical in every episode for a given target; B, two or three specs from only ProcTHOR's four types, because the variety is cosmetic and "one bedroom, two bathrooms" is an unrealistic house.

Weights are a draft and can be tuned before training starts.

#### Where the object is when the map is wrong — Option B: drawn from the co-occurrence table (locked 2026-11-16)

Claim-first order (from Point 1): the map claims a room; with probability r the object is there; otherwise it is in one of the other rooms of this house, drawn from the table weights for the current target, renormalized over those remaining rooms (0.5 floor). Refined by the 3c start-room lock: "other rooms" excludes both the claimed room and the start room, so a wrong claim puts the object in one of the remaining three rooms. The value-iteration solver and the Bayes baselines use the same table, so the optimum stays exact. A config flag `placement = table | uniform` exists from day one; uniform is just the table with every weight set to 1, so it costs no extra code. One E1 ablation row (an ablation reruns an experiment with one ingredient switched off) is trained and tested with `uniform` to separate trust in the map from commonsense. In the main results PPO-NoPrior becomes the "commonsense only" agent, so the PPO-Conf minus PPO-NoPrior gap is the value the map adds on top of commonsense. Worked example (written before the start-room lock, so it spreads over four rooms): target book, two-bedroom house, map claims living room, r = 0.6; each bedroom gets 0.4 × 4/9.5 ≈ 0.17, bathroom about 0.04, kitchen about 0.02 (uniform would give each 0.10), so after an empty living room each bedroom holds 0.42 of what remains.

Computation (Khaled's concern, answered): sampling the room is one random number per episode (microseconds); value iteration over 160 states × at most 5 actions costs about 1 ms per episode, about 1 s for all 1,000 E1 test episodes, and is needed equally under Option A. The table changes the numbers, not the amount of computation. Dependence on the table is bounded: the table only acts in the 1 − r share of episodes where the map is wrong, the uniform flag always gives the table-free version, and the ablation row turns "why these numbers?" into a measured result. Optional at any time without changing this lock: debug with the table of ones first (the staged Option C that was offered).

Scope of `placement = uniform` (Khaled, 2026-11-17, answering Claude Code OPEN_QUESTIONS Q1): uniform replaces the table by ones in all three places it is used: the wrong-claim object draw, the table branch of the λ claim draw (3c), and the habit feature (4a). So the ablation world has no commonsense at all, and in it the habit feature is the same for every room.

Why (Khaled's reason, corrected and recorded): Khaled said "it now learns commonsense". Correction: Option B puts realistic commonsense regularities into the world, but the policy can only learn to use them if Point 4 shows them to it. Recorded reason: with Option B the world contains realistic commonsense regularities, so the policy can learn to combine two knowledge sources, the map's claim and commonsense about where the object usually is, deciding from experience how much weight each deserves; this requires Point 4 to include, per room, that room's table weight for the current target, normalized over the house (done in 4a). Rejected: A, uniform other room (unrealistic, wastes the realistic topology, makes PPO-NoPrior nearly identical to the nearest-room explorer; kept as the ablation setting); C, staged (uniform until the 20 November checkpoint, then table), not chosen as the plan, though the flag keeps it available.

Clarifications discussed after the lock (2026-11-16). Habit does not override the map by design: in claim-first order the claimed room holds the object with probability r, so for the towel in a two-bedroom house with the map claiming the living room, P(living) = c and P(bathroom) = 0.615(1 − c) (computed over four other rooms, before the start-room lock; with the start room excluded the bathroom share rises slightly, for example to 8/12 ≈ 0.67 of 1 − c when the robot starts in the kitchen, moving the crossover from about 0.38 to about 0.40); the map wins above that crossover (lower still when the claimed room is closer). A policy that always goes to the bathroom for towels makes a measurable mistake (visible in regret and in the E2 prior-swap test); the target behavior is "follow the map when confident, fall back to habit when not". Learning "towels are usually in bathrooms" is not harmful memorization, because the rule holds in every training and test house. The confidence c and the habit weights are different quantities: c is per claim and per episode, attached to one room, and can be dishonest when κ ≠ 1; the habit weight is per target and room type, covers all rooms, and never lies; they agree in some episodes and disagree in others, and after the claimed room is found empty only habit can rank the remaining rooms. They are kept as separate inputs rather than merged into one posterior per room, because merging assumes c is honest (that is Bayes-nominal) and would remove the policy's chance to learn to correct miscalibration (H3).

### Point 3c: How the prior map is produced and corrupted (complete, 2026-11-16)

#### How the claimed room is chosen — Option C: mixture of table and uniform with λ = 0.5 (locked 2026-11-16)

Each episode, a coin with probability λ = 0.5 decides how the claimed room is picked: with probability 1 − λ it is drawn from the current target's table weights over this house's five rooms; with probability λ it is drawn uniformly (each room 0.2). This λ coin only affects what the map says; where the object goes when the claim is wrong is always the table (3b). λ is a fixed design setting reported in Methods, not learned (structurally like ε-greedy from M4-3, but used by the environment to generate hints, not by the agent to pick actions; not related to Double Q-learning, which keeps two learned Q-tables). λ = 0 recovers Option B and λ = 1 recovers Option A, so both remain available without new code; an E1 sensitivity row varying λ (for example 0, 0.5, 1) checks whether the main conclusion survives a different error model.

Numbers for the towel in a two-bedroom house (claim distribution): pure table gives bathroom 0.59, each bedroom 0.15, kitchen 0.07, living room 0.04; λ = 0.5 gives bathroom 0.40, each bedroom 0.17, kitchen 0.14, living room 0.12. Sharp disagreements (map claims a room habit rates as unlikely: kitchen or living room) rise from 11% to 26%. Correction recorded: under the pure table the map already points away from the bathroom 41% of the time, so "almost always agree" overstated the problem; the real issue is that sharp disagreements, which test the crossover, are rare.

Why (recorded by Claude, since Khaled gave the choice without a reason; Khaled may correct it): Option C is the only option that keeps habit meaningful and also produces enough sharp map-versus-habit disagreements to train and test the crossover; λ = 0 and λ = 1 recover the other two options for a sensitivity row. Rejected: A, uniform claims (because the object sits in the claimed room with probability r, at low ρ objects would follow the claims and be spread uniformly, making the table and the 3b lock pointless); B, claims from the table only (realistic, but sharp disagreements are rare, about 11% for the towel, so the policy sees few cases where the map should beat strong habit and trust becomes hard to separate from habit).

Khaled's concern "aren't we relying too much on probabilities?" (answered): an unreliable map is itself a probability statement, so the randomness is the research question, not a side effect; it is confined to the draws at reset; the known generator is what allows the exact value-iteration optimum and regret, which Castelo-Branco could not compute in Habitat. Limitation for the Discussion: real semantic map errors are not generated by this Beta, λ and table recipe, so conclusions hold for this error model; every dial (ρ, κ, λ, table versus uniform) is a single number with an off switch, setting λ = 1 with the uniform table gives the simplest world for debugging, and "real map error models" is stated future work linked to the thesis.

#### How many rooms the map claims — Option A: a single claimed room with one confidence c (locked 2026-11-16)

The map says exactly one thing per episode, for example "towel: living room, 0.55": one claimed room, one true reliability r, one displayed confidence c, one coin deciding whether the claim is right. The other rooms are ranked only by the habit weights.

Why (recorded by Claude, since Khaled gave the choice without a reason; Khaled may correct it): it matches the Point 1 dials exactly; the threshold c* stays a single, explainable number; it keeps the map and habit as clearly separate knowledge sources, which 3b was built around; it is a recognizable simplification of real semantic maps, many of which store for each place the most likely label plus its confidence. Full-distribution maps are stated future work linked to the thesis. Rejected: B, a full probability per room (closest to real semantic maps, but needs a separate model of how the whole vector goes wrong, κ must become a temperature on five numbers, r loses its single meaning, c* stops being one threshold, and the map's numbers for the other rooms duplicate the habit weights, merging map and habit); C, top-2 claims with two confidences (a second coin and a second confidence in every feature row for little gain in what can be measured).

#### Spread of r — Option B: Beta concentration n = 10 (locked 2026-11-16)

r ~ Beta(α, β) with α = 10(1 − ρ) and β = 10ρ, so the mean is 1 − ρ. A Beta distribution is a distribution over numbers between 0 and 1; α and β act like counts of past successes and failures, and n = α + β sets how tightly r clusters around the mean. At ρ = 0.4 this is Beta(6, 4): standard deviation about 0.15, r mostly between 0.45 and 0.75. At ρ = 0.8 it is Beta(2, 8). ρ = 0 is handled as r = 1 (a perfect map, c = 1), since β = 0 is not a valid Beta and logit(1) is infinite.

Why (recorded by Claude, since Khaled chose the recommended option without a reason; Khaled may correct it): the ρ conditions stay distinct, so the E1 curves keep their shape, while c still varies inside each condition, so trust by confidence level can be learned and measured in E2. Rejected: A, no spread (r = 1 − ρ exactly; every claim in a test condition looks identical, E2 confidence bins collapse at fixed ρ, and it contradicts the Point 1 lock); C, n = 2 (at ρ = 0.4, Beta(1.2, 0.8), standard deviation about 0.28, r spans almost 0 to 1, neighboring ρ conditions overlap heavily and the E1 curves flatten). An interactive dot plot of sampled r values for the three options was shown in chat.

#### Start-room rule — Option C: start drawn before the object, among the four non-claimed rooms (locked 2026-11-16)

The start room is drawn uniformly among the four rooms other than the claimed room, before the object is placed; the start room counts as already searched. If the r coin says the claim is correct, the object is in the claimed room; otherwise it is placed among the remaining three rooms (not the claimed room, not the start room) by the table weights renormalized over them.

Why (recorded by Claude, since Khaled chose the recommended option without a reason; Khaled may correct it): no episode is won or settled at time zero, every episode has a real first decision, and P(claim correct) = r exactly, so the exact optimum stays exact. Rejected: A, start anywhere (some episodes are won at time zero, and when the start is the claimed room the claim is settled before any decision); B, start anywhere except the object's room and the claimed room, drawn after placement (leaks information: a given start room is chosen with probability 1/4 when the claim is correct but 1/3 when it is wrong, so the start position itself tilts the odds toward "map wrong"; this was Claude's own earlier proposal, corrected before locking).

#### Final episode generation recipe (Point 3 complete)

All randomness happens at reset; after it everything is deterministic (precomputed distances, deterministic camera, reward = minus meters). 1) Draw a house from the pool. 2) Draw a target uniformly from the eight. 3) Draw r ~ Beta(10(1 − ρ), 10ρ) (r = 1 if ρ = 0) and display c = σ(κ·logit(r)). 4) Flip the λ coin and pick the claimed room (table weights or uniform). 5) Draw the start room uniformly among the four non-claimed rooms. 6) Flip the r coin: if correct, the object is in the claimed room; otherwise draw it among the remaining three rooms by the table. Worked example: house 1,742, towel, ρ = 0.4 gives r = 0.55 and c = 0.55 at κ = 1; λ coin lands uniform and the die picks the living room; start room is the kitchen; r coin lands wrong; the remaining weights bathroom 8, bedroom A 2, bedroom B 2 (sum 12) put the towel in the bathroom with 8/12 ≈ 0.67, each bedroom 0.17; it lands in the bathroom. The robot knows only: map says living room with 0.55, the habit weights, distances, and that the kitchen is already searched.

### Point 4: Observation (complete, 2026-11-17)

#### 4a: Per-room feature row — Option A: minimal five-feature row, no global features (locked 2026-11-17)

The observation is 5 rows, one per room, in an order shuffled every episode, never containing room IDs or room-type names. Each row has five numbers: 1) planned distance (A* on the occupancy map) from the robot's current room viewpoint to this room's viewpoint, divided by the largest room-to-room planned distance in this house, so it lies in [0, 1]; 2) searched flag (0 or 1); 3) claimed flag (1 only on the room the map claims); 4) displayed confidence c on the claimed row, 0 on every other row; 5) this room's co-occurrence weight for the current target, normalized over the rooms of this house (habit). c and habit stay separate inputs (see the 3b clarifications). No global features (such as rooms searched so far or meters spent): the true state is current room plus searched set, which the rows already contain, so the Markov property holds. Revisit only if Point 6 introduces a distance budget, in which case "meters spent" or "budget left" becomes part of the state.

Why (Khaled's reason): simplicity, and no need to complicate the project further. Rejected: B, adding global features (only needed with a budget; otherwise extra inputs the policy must learn to ignore); C, also adding each room's distance to the claimed room (helps "on the way" route planning but is partly redundant, since the network can combine distances itself).

#### 4b: Rooms only driven through — Option A: they do not count as searched (locked 2026-11-17)

Only the room the policy chose gets searched; the virtual camera checks for the target only on arrival at that room's viewpoint, in both the 2D simulator and Gazebo. Rooms the robot passes through stay unsearched. In the Gazebo demo this is explained as "the robot searches only where it decides to look".

Why (Khaled's reason): keep it simple, as in 4a; it also keeps the exact optimum exact, since the value-iteration state stays current room plus searched set and the next state depends only on the chosen room. Rejected: B, transit counts as searching (more realistic, but the solver needs per-path room coverage and partial-coverage rules); C, transit detection ends the episode but does not mark rooms searched (natural demo, but gives the policy free detections the solver does not count, so regret is no longer exact).

#### 4c: How the comparison variants are formed — Option A (locked 2026-11-17)

All three PPO agents share the same network and code and differ only by a fixed mask that zeroes inputs (inputs are zeroed, not deleted). PPO-Conf ("confidence"): sees everything, the claimed room and its confidence c. PPO-Binary: sees the claimed flag in every episode but c is always 0, so it knows where the map points but never how sure the map is; it can only learn one average level of trust over the training mix of ρ. PPO-NoPrior ("no prior map"): claimed flag and c both always 0; it sees only distances, searched flags and habit weights, so it is the "commonsense only" agent. The names describe information removed step by step: claim plus confidence, claim only, nothing. PPO-Conf minus PPO-Binary measures the value of the confidence number; PPO-Binary minus PPO-NoPrior measures the value of the claim itself. PPO is Proximal Policy Optimization: a neural network outputs the policy directly (the probability of choosing each room), and each update changes the policy only a little from the previous one to keep training stable.

Why (Khaled's reason): the most logical choice, and it does not leak confidence information for the model to use. Rejected: B, show the claim only when c ≥ 0.5 (mimics real thresholded semantic maps but leaks one bit of confidence, so the PPO-Conf versus PPO-Binary gap no longer measures the full value of c; can be mentioned in the Discussion as a realistic middle case); C, claimed flag with c fixed at the training average (about 0.6; the same information as A in a different form).

### Point 5: Actions (complete, 2026-11-17)

#### 5a: Choosing an already-searched room — Option A: action masking (locked 2026-11-17)

Each decision the policy chooses one of the five rows, but only unsearched rooms are allowed. The environment gives the policy a mask (five yes/no values, one per row in the shuffled order) marking the unsearched rooms; before the network's room scores are turned into probabilities, the scores of masked rooms are pushed so low that their probability is exactly zero. The start room is searched from the start, so it is masked at the first decision; after each failed search one more room is masked. An episode therefore has at most 4 decisions and can never stall, and the policy's choices are exactly the actions of the value-iteration optimum ("go search an unsearched room"), so regret compares like with like. All baselines obey the same mask: the random baseline picks uniformly among unsearched rooms, and the nearest-unexplored explorer already does by definition. The same mask is applied in Gazebo. Implementation note for Point 7: a ready-made masked PPO exists (`MaskablePPO` in `sb3-contrib`). Reference: Huang and Ontañón, "A Closer Look at Invalid Action Masking in Policy Gradient Algorithms", FLAIRS 2022 (masking learns faster and more reliably than penalizing invalid actions).

The object is never in the start room (Khaled's requirement, 2026-11-17). This already follows from the 3c recipe (step 6 places a wrong-claim object only among the rooms that are neither claimed nor the start room, and the start room is never the claimed room) and is covered by a test; Claude Code adds an explicit runtime check in `generate_episode` so the rule cannot break silently. Together with masking, no episode is ever won at time zero and no reward can come from the start room.

Refined by 6b: the episode is capped at 3 decisions, so an episode now has at most 3 decisions, not 4 (the fourth was forced anyway, since only one unsearched room is left).

Why (Khaled's choice, reason recorded by Claude; Khaled may correct it): it removes the zero-cost "stay in the current room" trap that a meter-based reward would otherwise create, keeps episodes at most 4 decisions, keeps the comparison with the exact optimum clean, and spends training only on the trust decision. Rejected: B, allowed with a fixed penalty (wasted early training, an extra number to justify, needs a decision cap); C, allowed with no effect (re-choosing the current room costs 0 meters, so without a success bonus or per-decision cost the agent could learn never to move; ties Point 5 to Point 6 and needs a decision cap).

#### 5b: How an episode ends — Option A: automatic success on detection, no extra actions (locked 2026-11-17)

On arrival at the chosen room's viewpoint the virtual camera checks for the target. If it is there, the episode ends with success; if not, the room is marked searched and the policy decides again. There is no "declare found" action and no "give up" action, so the action set stays at the five masked room choices. Since the target always exists and detection is deterministic, every episode ends in success within at most 4 decisions, and the only difference between policies is the planned meters spent until the target is found, which is exactly what regret against the optimum measures. The value-iteration state stays current room plus searched set.

Why (Khaled's choice, reason recorded by Claude; Khaled may correct it): it matches the locked world (object always present, perfect detection), keeps the action set minimal, and makes "meters until found" the single quantity every method is compared on. Rejected: B, explicit "declare found" action (the STOP action of ObjectNav benchmarks, Batra et al., "ObjectNav Revisited", 2020; needed there because detection is uncertain, trivial here, and adds a sixth action, a new failure mode and a larger optimum unrelated to trust); C, automatic success plus a "give up" action (only meaningful with an absent object or a distance budget; with a minus-meters reward, quitting would beat searching unless Point 6 adds a failure penalty). Revisit C only if Point 6 adds a distance budget.

Refined by 6b: with the 3-decision cap, the episode ends with success on detection, or with failure if the third decision misses (the object was in the room left for last). There is still no "declare found" or "give up" action.

### Point 6: Reward (complete, 2026-11-17)

#### 6a: Units of the reward — Option B: planned meters divided by the house's largest room-to-room distance (locked 2026-11-17)

Each decision's reward is minus the planned A* distance of that move, divided by the largest room-to-room planned distance in this house (the same scale as the 4a distance feature), so a single trip costs at most 1 "house-width". All results (regret, SPL, distances) are still reported in meters, by multiplying back by each house's stored largest distance. Scaling by a positive constant within an episode does not change which choices are best in that house.

Why (Khaled accepted the recommendation): it is the only option where the reward is in the same units the policy observes, so identical observations lead to identical returns; returns stay near the ±1 range neural networks learn best in. Rejected: A, raw meters (returns depend on house size, which the normalized observation hides, so the value network, PPO's second output that estimates the expected return like V(s) in M3, gets noisy targets; returns of tens of meters); C, meters divided by a fixed constant such as 10 m (near ±1, but the same house-size mismatch as A).

#### 6b: Budget and other reward terms — cap of 3 decisions with a failure penalty P = 1 (locked 2026-11-17)

An episode is capped at 3 decisions. It ends with success when the virtual camera detects the target on arrival; if the third decision misses (the object was in the room left for last), it ends with failure and a reward of −1 is added. There is no per-decision cost and no other reward term. Return = −(total scaled distance) − 1·[missed]. The number of decisions made equals the number of searched rooms minus one, so it is already in the observation and in the value-iteration state; 4a needs no global feature and the exact optimum stays exact and tiny. Metrics: success rate (now meaningful), SPL (Success weighted by Path Length, Anderson et al., "On Evaluation of Embodied Navigation Agents", 2018; failures count as 0), decision regret, plus "success within 1 decision" as an extra trust metric (finding the object on the first try is the clearest sign of correct trust).

Khaled's idea (cap at 3 so that failures exist) and the reason for P = 1: a failure must cost something, because with a distance-only reward a missed episode skips the last trip and would be cheaper than a success, so the agent would learn to prefer failing. P = 1 equals the longest possible single trip (one house-width), so failing on purpose to save a trip never pays, and it is small enough that distance and failure risk both shape the decision, which keeps the trust threshold c* distance-dependent (H2). A larger P (for example +10) was rejected: failure would dominate, the agent would search the three most likely rooms almost regardless of distance, c* would become nearly distance-independent, and H2 would have little to show; no P = 10 sensitivity row (Khaled's choice). With a cap, "+P on success" and "−P on failure" lead to exactly the same choices (they differ by a constant added to every return), so the report may write the reward either way; this file keeps the failure-penalty form. With the cap, choosing the order of rooms also chooses which room to give up on, a trust-relevant decision (with low confidence, should the claimed room be the one skipped?). The cap is a dial: 4 recovers the no-budget world, 2 is harsher.

Rejected: no budget (every episode succeeds, success rate always 100%); a distance budget (distance-so-far would have to enter the observation, reopening 4a, and the 160-state exact optimum would become a continuous problem); budget in evaluation only (agent never trained for it; kept only as the extra "success within 1 decision" metric); a per-decision cost (an extra number to justify, and the camera check is instant, so there is no search time to model); rewarding only the find without a distance cost (distance would stop mattering, removing the distance-dependent trust that H2 is about).

#### 6c: Discount factor — Option A: γ = 1 (locked 2026-11-17)

No discounting: the return is exactly −(total scaled distance) − 1·[missed], which is what the value-iteration optimum minimizes and what the results report. Episodes always end within 3 decisions, and Sutton and Barto note that γ = 1 is valid for episodic tasks that always end. This settles the "γ of 0.99 or 1" left open in the Agreements section.

Why (Khaled accepted after discussion): it makes the training objective, the exact optimum and the reported metrics the same number. Khaled's question "doesn't γ < 1 make the robot find the object as early as possible?" (answered): no; discounting pushes an agent to act early only when rewards are positive; all our rewards are costs, and discounting a cost makes later costs smaller, so γ < 1 nudges the agent to postpone costs; finding early is already enforced with γ = 1 because every extra search adds its trip, and a miss adds 1. γ = 1.01 (later costs count more) was also rejected: it rewards fewer decisions even at the price of more meters, which trains a different goal than the reported one, does openly what a per-decision cost would do, and γ outside [0, 1] is nonstandard. Rejected: B, γ = 0.99 (the PPO library default; makes third-decision costs about 2% cheaper, a small mismatch with the undiscounted metrics); C, γ = 0.9 (noticeably short-sighted).

### Point 7: RL algorithm and network (complete, 2026-11-17)

#### 7a: Algorithm — Option A: `MaskablePPO` from `sb3-contrib` (locked 2026-11-17)

The learning algorithm is the library version of PPO with action masking: `MaskablePPO` from `sb3-contrib`, the add-on package to Stable-Baselines3. The environment provides an `action_masks()` function returning the five yes/no values of 5a. How it works, in plain words (explained to Khaled 2026-11-17): the policy network outputs one raw score (logit) per room; the scores of searched rooms are set to minus infinity; a softmax turns the remaining scores into probabilities, so masked rooms get probability exactly zero; the room is sampled from those probabilities; the same mask is applied when PPO computes its update, so the network is never trained on actions it could not have taken; each update is clipped so the policy changes only a little (the "proximal" part). CPU only; runs on Ubuntu and Windows. Reference: Huang and Ontañón, FLAIRS 2022 (already cited in 5a).

Why (Khaled's choice, reason recorded by Claude; Khaled may correct it): a tested, citable implementation with masking built in, about twenty lines of training code, and no risk of PPO bugs before the 20 November checkpoint. Khaled must still be able to explain what happens inside (logits, mask, softmax, clipped update), but does not defend hand-written algorithm code. Rejected: B, PPO written from scratch in one file in the CleanRL style (every line explainable and custom networks easy, but a day or more of work and bug risk before the checkpoint); C, a different algorithm such as DQN (closer to tabular Q-learning from the course, but would reopen the locked Point 1 research question, which names PPO).

#### 7b: Network shape — Option A: flat network (locked 2026-11-17)

The 25 observation numbers (5 shuffled rows × 5 features, 4a) are fed in as one list. The policy network has two hidden layers of 64 units with tanh and 5 outputs (one score per row); the value network (the second network, estimating the expected return like V(s) in M3) has the same hidden layers and one output. These are the SB3 `MlpPolicy` defaults, so no custom policy code is needed. The network depends on row position, which is acceptable only because the row order is shuffled every episode (4a, 3a note): the network must learn the same rule for every slot, and the shuffle plus 5,000 training houses give it enough data to do so. The three variants (4c) use this same network with zeroed inputs.

Why (Khaled's choice, reason recorded by Claude; Khaled may correct it): it works out of the box with 7a, is the easiest network to explain, and is enough for 25 inputs and 5 outputs. Optional extra, only if time allows after the checkpoint: Option C as an extra row and Discussion point on order-independent designs. Rejected as the main network: B, a shared per-room scorer (one small network with shared weights scores each row on its own; order-independent by construction and works for any number of rooms, but each room is scored without seeing the others, while the best choice depends on what remains, so it may cap how close the policy gets to the optimum); C, a per-room scorer with a house summary (each row's embedding is averaged into one summary vector that is attached to every row before scoring; the Deep Sets design, Zaheer et al., NeurIPS 2017; order-independent and uses the whole house, but needs about 40–60 lines of custom SB3 policy code, about half a day, because SB3's standard output layer mixes the rows).

#### 7c: PPO settings — Option B: SB3 defaults tuned to this problem (locked 2026-11-17)

γ = 1 (6c; overrides the library default 0.99). GAE λ = 1.0: the advantage (how much better an action turned out than the value network expected) is the full Monte Carlo return minus the value estimate, as in M4-1; GAE λ is PPO's own setting for mixing Monte Carlo returns with one-step TD targets, like TD(λ), and is unrelated to our λ = 0.5 of 3c. Entropy bonus 0.01: a small reward for keeping the room probabilities spread out, a smooth version of ε in ε-greedy, so the policy does not lock onto "always follow the map" early and can discover that distrust pays at low confidence. 8 vectorized environments (8 copies of the 2D simulator stepping side by side, so each update gets more data). Everything else stays at the SB3 defaults: learning rate 3e-4 (step size of each network update), rollout length 2048 decisions per environment before each update, minibatch 64, 10 epochs (passes over each batch of data), clip range 0.2 (limit on how far one update can move the policy).

Why (Khaled's choice, reason recorded by Claude; Khaled may correct it): with episodes of at most 3 decisions the extra variance of full Monte Carlo returns is small, and GAE λ = 1 keeps the advantage in terms Khaled already knows; the entropy bonus protects the exploration that the trust result depends on; the rest are well-tested defaults that need no justification beyond citation. Rejected: A, plain SB3 defaults (γ = 0.99 contradicts 6c, GAE λ = 0.95 and no entropy bonus); C, a 3 × 3 search over learning rate and entropy bonus on the validation houses before any real result (nine extra runs; kept as a fallback only if training curves look unstable).

#### 7d: Training length and seeds — Option B with fallback to A (locked 2026-11-17)

Every run trains for a fixed 1 million decisions (about 400,000 episodes, so each of the 5,000 training houses is seen about 80 times with different targets, claims and confidences). Every 50,000 decisions training pauses and the policy is evaluated on a fixed set of validation-house episodes; the checkpoint with the lowest validation regret is the one tested, and the validation curve (regret against decisions trained) is the learning curve in the report. Seeds: 5 per main variant (PPO-Conf, PPO-Binary, PPO-NoPrior), 3 for each extra row (PPO-Conf at κ = 3, the uniform-placement ablation, the λ sensitivity row), about 21 runs. A seed fixes all the randomness of one run (starting network weights, the rooms sampled during training, the order of training episodes); results are reported as the mean over seeds with a confidence interval. The first run (the 20 November go/no-go run, one seed of PPO-Conf against random) measures the real time per million decisions. If it is under about 20 minutes, Option B stands; if it is much slower, fall back to Option A (3 seeds everywhere, about 15 runs). Runs can go in parallel on separate CPU cores. Claude's unmeasured estimate: 10–20 minutes per run.

Why (Khaled accepted the recommendation after asking for more explanation): the must-have result H1 compares agents that may differ only a little (for example PPO-Conf against PPO-Binary at middle ρ); with 3 seeds the confidence intervals can overlap and the crossover becomes "maybe", with 5 they shrink by roughly a quarter. Paired evaluation removes noise from test episodes but not from training seeds; only more seeds do that. Agarwal et al., "Deep Reinforcement Learning at the Edge of the Statistical Precipice", NeurIPS 2021, shows how unreliable 3-seed comparisons are. A fixed 1M for every run makes "every agent got the same training" automatic. Rejected: A as the plan (kept as the fallback; valid but wider intervals); C, train until validation regret stops improving with a 2M cap (less compute, but runs end at different lengths and the report must argue fairness).

### Point 8: Baselines and optimal reference (complete, 2026-11-17)

Khaled took 8a after a full explanation and asked Claude to lock its recommendation for 8b–8d. A worked example with a backward-induction diagram and an interactive optimum explorer was shown in chat on 2026-11-17.

#### 8a: What the optimum knows — Option A: the true reliability r (locked 2026-11-17)

For every test episode the optimum is the exact solution of that episode's room-level MDP: state = current room plus searched set; action = search an unsearched room; reward = minus the scaled distance, plus −1 if the third search misses; transition = found with the probability that the object is in the chosen room given that it was not in any room searched so far; solved by value iteration (M3-4). The room probabilities use the true r: r for the claimed room, 0 for the start room, and 1 − r split over the other three rooms by the table weights, exactly as the 3c recipe places the object. The optimum does not know where the object is; it minimizes expected cost. With κ fixed per run or condition, c = σ(κ·logit(r)) can be inverted (r = σ(logit(c)/κ)), so a policy that sees c can in principle match this optimum whenever training and test use the same κ. Because the only thing a failed search reveals is "not here", every policy in one episode is a fixed search order, so the optimum also equals the best of the 24 three-room orders.

Why (Khaled's choice after the explanation): one common yardstick for every agent, baseline and ρ value, matching the C3 contribution; the regret gaps between variants measure the value of information directly. Rejected: B, the displayed c taken at face value (not optimal when κ ≠ 1; kept as the Bayes-nominal baseline); C, A plus information-limited optima per variant (splits regret into information gap and learning gap, about half a day of work; kept as an optional E4 stretch).

#### 8b: How regret is measured — Option A: expected decision regret, reported in meters (locked 2026-11-17)

At every decision, regret = Q*(s, best room) − Q*(s, agent's room), using the optimum's values from 8a; an episode's regret is the sum over the decisions actually made, converted to meters by multiplying by the house's largest distance (6a). Luck is removed: a decision is judged by what was knowable when it was made, not by where the object turned out to be; it is never negative, has low noise, and points to the specific wrong decision (needed in E2). Its average equals the agent's expected cost minus the optimum's expected cost. Worked example (towel, start kitchen, map says living room, r = 0.55): going to the bathroom first scores 1.57 − 1.09 = 0.48 house-widths of regret wherever the towel is.

Why (Claude's recommendation, accepted by Khaled): it matches the research question (correct trust, not luck), and its one-line explanation fits the presentation. Rejected: B, realized regret (agent's actual cost minus the optimum's actual cost in the same episode; intuitive but noisy, can be negative, needs many more episodes); C, both (realized distance is already visible through average driven distance and SPL).

#### 8c: Checking the optimum is correct — Option C: brute-force test plus tabular Q-learning (E0) (locked 2026-11-17)

Two checks. 1) An automated test: on a few thousand random episodes, the value-iteration optimum must equal the best of all 24 three-room search orders, found by brute force. 2) E0: tabular Q-learning (M4-2) on a fixed set of episode configurations (house, target, claim, r, start room) with state = current room plus searched set must converge to the value-iteration Q-values and pick the same rooms. E0 links the project to the course and supports a presentation point: tabular RL can reach the optimum when the state is a small table, and what PPO adds is generalization across houses and confidence values.

Why (Claude's recommendation, accepted by Khaled): the brute-force test catches bugs in the solver at almost no cost; E0 is the proposal's sanity check and costs little. Rejected: A, Q-learning only (slower and noisier as a bug check); B, brute force only (loses the course link and E0).

#### 8d: Baselines — Option A: four baselines; Bayes-oracle merged into the optimum (locked 2026-11-17)

Random: picks uniformly among unsearched rooms. Nearest-unexplored explorer: always goes to the nearest unsearched room (ignores the map and habit). Greedy map follower: searches the claimed room first, then the nearest unsearched room (follows the map blindly, ignores confidence and habit). Bayes-nominal: the value-iteration optimum computed with the displayed c taken as r (identical to the optimum when κ = 1; under miscalibration it trusts a dishonest number, which is what H3 tests against). All obey the 5a mask and the 3-decision cap. The proposal's Bayes-oracle (uses the true r) is the 8a optimum itself (regret 0 by definition), so it is the reference line, not a separate baseline. Worked-example regrets (r = 0.55, house largest distance 10 m): random 4.1 m, nearest-unexplored 4.0 m, greedy follower 2.1 m, Bayes-nominal 0 m.

Why (Claude's recommendation, accepted by Khaled): each baseline isolates one behavior the trust question needs: no strategy (random), distance only (nearest), blind trust (follower, the crossover partner in H1), and correct use of a confidence that may lie (Bayes-nominal, H3). Rejected: B, adding a habit-greedy baseline (overlaps with PPO-NoPrior's role as the commonsense-only agent); C, only random and the greedy follower (loses the distance-only reference and the H3 comparison).

### Engineering parameters: house generator, grid, camera and development house (answers Claude Code OPEN_QUESTIONS Q2–Q5, locked 2026-11-18)

Guiding rule (Khaled, 2026-11-18): every step takes the simplest option that works, and no new dial is added unless a hypothesis needs it, so that it always stays clear what influences what. None of the settings below touches the trust question; they only have to produce sensible houses and paths.

Q2, house shape. The footprint is a plain rectangle, width and depth each drawn uniformly between 8 and 12 m; no L or T corner cuts (optional in 3a, left out for simplicity). The rectangle is first cut across its longer side into the public zone (kitchen, living, plus dining if present) and the private zone (bedroom, bathroom, plus the second bedroom or office); each zone is then cut into its rooms, again across its longer side. Area shares follow growth weights: living 3, kitchen 2, dining 2, bedroom 2, office 1.5, bathroom 1. A room narrower than 2 m makes the house rejected and redrawn. Every connection is a 1.0 m door centred on the shared wall, including kitchen–living (3a allows an open connection there but does not require it). Doors form a tree, so there is exactly one route between any two rooms: every room is linked to a public-zone neighbour when it touches one, otherwise to a private neighbour other than the bathroom; the bathroom gets exactly one door; a house that cannot be connected under these rules is rejected. Simplification of 3a: no entrance is built, since the robot starts inside the start room drawn by the 3c recipe and a front door has no role in the 2D simulator or in Gazebo.

Q3, grid, A* and viewpoints. Occupancy grid with 0.10 m cells; walls 0.20 m thick; A* in 8 directions with diagonal steps costing √2 cells; before planning, walls are inflated by the robot radius of 0.20 m, leaving a 0.6 m passage through each door. Each room's viewpoint is the free (after inflation) cell nearest the room centre. The 5 × 5 planned-distance table is computed once per house when the pool is generated and saved to disk. 0.10 m rather than 0.05 m keeps the grid four times smaller, so generating the 5,000-house pool takes minutes in plain Python, with distances still accurate to about 10 cm.

Q4, camera and object position. On arrival at a viewpoint the target is detected if it lies within 1.5 m of the viewpoint with a clear line of sight (ray cast on the occupancy grid), which is the same as the robot turning a full circle on the spot. The object is placed on a random free cell of its room that meets this condition, so detection is certain in the object's room and impossible elsewhere, exactly as the optimum assumes. The object's exact spot changes no distance, since every cost is measured between viewpoints. In Gazebo the robot does the turn on the spot for the demo.

Q5, development house. The development house is generator seed 0, the first house of the training pool, so development runs through the same code that builds the whole pool. Claude Code plots it and Khaled checks it by eye; if it is odd (for example cramped), the next few seeds are inspected and one of them is used instead, still from the generator.

Robot. The Gazebo robot is the minimal two-wheeled differential-drive robot with lidar from Point 2 (no TurtleBot). Its footprint is a circle of radius 0.20 m, which sets the wall inflation above. Wheel base, lidar model and speed limits are settled when the Gazebo part starts, since they do not affect training.

### Agreements that follow from these decisions

Gazebo results never update the policy. If simulator calibration is ever needed, Gazebo work is split into a calibration set (about 20 Nav2 goals without the policy) and a test set that is never fed back. Checkpoints are selected on a validation set of 2D houses (separate seed range from training and test). Observations are computed by identical code in both simulators (same A* on the same occupancy map, shared room and viewpoint points). Detection is a virtual camera: a field-of-view cone plus line-of-sight ray casting on the occupancy map, the same function in both simulators, using Gazebo's ground-truth pose. The task is find only, no manipulation; success means the target is visible within about 1.5 m with clear line of sight. One decision is one step (a semi-MDP); γ = 1 per decision (locked in 6c). Domain randomization of driving is out of the core plan; it returns only as Discussion material if the end-to-end gap is large. In Gazebo the house geometry is fixed per test house, but each episode changes the target, the object positions, the prior map, the confidence and the start pose. "Explore" has two meanings to keep distinct in the presentation: searching rooms the map does not point to (task sense) and trying non-greedy actions during training (RL sense).

Added 2026-11-16: drawing a house per episode from a fixed pool is part of the initial-state distribution, so the MDP stays fixed and stationary (like the dealt cards in Sutton and Barto's Blackjack example); the house is visible to the policy through its features, so the Markov property holds. Every method, seed and ρ value is evaluated on the identical test houses and episode seeds (paired evaluation, also called common random numbers). Optional extra result: a policy trained in one house and tested on unseen houses, to show memorization directly. Hints are random per episode but structured: r drawn from Beta(10(1 − ρ), 10ρ), the claim correct with probability r, c = σ(κ·logit(r)); κ is fixed per training run or test condition, not per episode; the claimed room is chosen by the λ mixture, and the map claims exactly one room (3c). The policy learns trust from returns: c is an input, so different confidence levels are different states (like a separate bandit per confidence level), the meter-based reward makes the threshold c* distance-dependent, and the policy's own random sampling (RL exploration) is what lets it discover that distrust pays at low confidence.

Consequence for Point 5: actions must be high level (room-level "go search room k"), not primitive grid moves. To decide at Point 5: what happens when the policy picks an already-searched room (masking, penalty, or allowed), and whether "declare found" is an explicit action or success is automatic on detection at the viewpoint.

Consequence for Points 6 and 9: the target always exists and detection is deterministic, so any agent that searches every room eventually succeeds; success rate alone says little. The quality measures are decision regret and SPL, or success within a distance or decision budget set at Point 6. If Point 6 adds a budget, revisit the 4a decision on global features. (Resolved in 6b: a 3-decision cap with a −1 failure penalty, so failures exist and success rate is meaningful; no global feature is needed because the decision count is already in the searched flags.)
